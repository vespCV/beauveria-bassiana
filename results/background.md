# Background

Catch-infect-release starts with a hornet picking up *Beauveria bassiana* (Bb) conidia. What happens next is a cuticle infection, then possible death, sublethal change, immune clearance, or sporulation on a cadaver. This page is the fungus biology that the six research questions use.

## What Bb is

Bb was first described from silkworm larvae by Agostino Bassi (Singh2015). It occurs in soil and on insects in the aerial environment (Zimmermann2007). Singh2015 reviews entomopathogenic versus endophytic use and strain improvement. Commercial use is typically inundative application of aerial conidia from solid-state fermentation, in dry or liquid formulations (Mascarin2016). Isolates differ: some exploit a wide host range while others are more specific (Brodeur2012; Feng et al. 1994 cited therein). Singh2015 reviews Bb use beyond Lepidoptera.

Conidial surfaces carry a net negative charge and bind hydrophobic insect cuticle (Valero-Jimenez2016; Leite2022 citing the same surface chemistry). Ortiz-Urquiza2013 reviews adhesion and cuticle breach. Pedrini2013 places hydrocarbon oxidation in that surface attack. Pedrini2022 reviews secondary-metabolite genes during pathogenesis. Ma2024 reviews the three infection stages (pre-contact behaviour, penetration and haemolymph growth, emergence and sporulation) and social-insect hygiene after death.

Tang2025 reports fungal evasion of *Drosophila* immunity via a cathepsin pathway. That is a dipteran immune-proxy paper.

## Infection steps

Valero-Jimenez2016 splits infection into seven steps:

| Step | Process |
|---|---|
| 1 | Host adhesion |
| 2 | Germination |
| 3 | Cuticle degradation |
| 4 | Growth as blastospores |
| 5 | Host colonization and killing |
| 6 | Immune-response interactions |
| 7 | Hyphal extrusion and conidiation |

Bb metabolites cited as insecticidal or immunosuppressive include beauvericin, bassianolide, oosporein, tenellin and beauverolides (Ma2024; Leite2022).

## Abiotic limits

Efficacy tracks humidity, temperature, rainfall and solar UV (Mascarin2016). Optimum temperature for Bb is about 23–28 °C, minimum about 5–10 °C, maximum about 30–38 °C, isolate-dependent (Zimmermann2007). Spore germination on the cuticle and sporulation after death need high moisture; infections have still been recorded at 60–70% macrohumidity (Zimmermann2007). After simulated sunlight, Ignoffo and Garcia (1992) reported a half-life of Bb conidia of about 2 hours (Zimmermann2007). That figure is for that irradiation assay.

Oil formulations improve infection at low humidity, thermal stress tolerance and survival under solar radiation (Mascarin2016). UV-blockers proposed for conidia include titanium dioxide, silicon dioxide, clays and dye-clay complexes (Mascarin2016).

## Strain and product quality

Separate Botanigard batches differed in ethanol tolerance, pathogenicity, colony growth and spore production (Moore2026; full-text extract, strain-trait fields not filled from PDF). Recultured BotaniGard killed more pheretimoid earthworms than label-as-prescribed product; 1-year refrigerated spores showed little mortality (Nouri-Aiin2021). balEnce germinated poorly in one Weeks2016 trial (<20% in a second preliminary test) while BotaniGard ES and Mycotrol O killed house flies (Weeks2016).

ButtGoettel2000 reviews bioassay design for entomopathogenic fungi: formulation can extend storage and field persistence by shielding conidia from desiccation and UV; carrier choice can improve attachment and germination on the cuticle; mortality is dose-related; bioassays screen host range, virulence and field-competence barriers.

## *V. velutina* biology used as scene-setting

Monceau2013 reviews *V. velutina* as the first invasive Vespidae predator of honeybees in Europe: life cycle, overwintering, foraging and dispersal; impact on beekeeping and ecosystems; management options including trapping and biological control.

Liu2015 is a venomic transcriptome of *V. velutina*. FeasSanchez2019 counted winter nest composition in Galicia (three nests; adult, larval and pupal percentages in the abstract). Turchi2018 reviews biological and physical control options for *V. velutina nigrithorax* in Europe. Dalmon2019 found honey-bee viruses (including DWV-B) in hornets. Gabin-Garcia2021 found shared hymenopteran enteropathogens in *V. velutina* and native Vespidae. Kanzaki2023 reports nematode and Strepsiptera parasitism in Japanese *Vespa*. Kim2025 records *Xenos moutoni* and *X. oxyodontes* on 11 *V. velutina* workers in South Korea (first parasite record on this hornet there). Kosaka2026 maps *Sphaerularia vespae*, a queen-sterilizing nematode, across five native *Vespa* hosts in Japan. Villemant2015 reports mermithid nematodes in French *V. velutina*. Yang2026 links *Serratia marcescens* to larval mortality in *Vespa analis*. Those papers describe the nest as a shared pathogen compartment.

Rose1999 reviews pathogens of social wasps and colony hygiene (removal of sick individuals, isolation of dead, brood compensation, capped cells).
